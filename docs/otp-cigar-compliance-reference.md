# OTP Cigar Compliance Reference — State-by-State (Lower 48 + DC)

**⚠️ COMPLIANCE RESEARCH DRAFT — NOT LEGAL ADVICE.**
This document is a research draft compiled for planning purposes only. It is **not** a legal opinion and must **not** be relied upon to file any state registration, tax account, or license application. Tobacco tax and delivery-sale law changes frequently, and a share of the findings below are still marked **"Needs Confirmation"** because they could not be verified against a directly-fetched primary source (see *Methodology & Limitations* at the bottom). **Get sign-off from a tobacco-compliance attorney or compliance service before filing any state registration, tax account, or license application on the strength of this document.**

Prepared for: The Tobacco Center (Williamsport, PA) — planning for CONUS (lower 48 + DC) direct-to-consumer cigar shipping. PA is the home state and already holds a PA OTP license; PA is included below as a baseline reference, not as a target for "expansion."

**Revision note (2026‑09‑28):** a second verification pass closed most of the items originally flagged for priority follow‑up. The most significant correction: **Maryland** taxes premium cigars at **15%** of wholesale price (not 70%) and has a dedicated, cheap **Remote Tobacco Seller License** built for exactly this business — it moves from the single highest‑risk state in the first draft to one of the more favorable ones. Also resolved this pass: West Virginia's tax rate (confirmed 12%, not 51%), Kentucky's premium‑cigar rate cut (confirmed signed into law, 15%→6%), Rhode Island's mail‑shipment statute (age‑restricted, not a blanket ban), Arkansas's physical‑presence bar (confirmed, not just suspected), New Jersey's tax‑hike bill (confirmed dead), Massachusetts's cap question (confirmed no cap exists), and Kansas's licensing mechanics (fully specified). Details and updated sourcing are in each row below; see the *Priority follow‑up* section for what's still genuinely open.

## How to read this table

- **Sort order**: ascending by **illustrative effective cigar tax**, calculated on a **hypothetical $10.00 wholesale-price premium cigar** (a mid-to-upper-range premium cigar) so that percentage-of-wholesale, percentage-with-cap, and flat-per-cigar tax structures can be compared on one axis. This is a modeling simplification — real tax owed depends on the actual wholesale price of each SKU. Always recompute against actual invoice cost.
- **Efficacy score (1–9)**: my own judgment call, not a formula output. It weighs: (a) whether DTC shipping is confirmed legal, banned, or ambiguous; (b) licensing cost and complexity (fee, bond size, number of agencies, physical-presence requirements); (c) the tax burden from the column to its left; and (d) rough market-size potential for **slow, organic, zero-paid-advertising sales**. It does **not** account for actual demand data, competitor density, or shipping/logistics cost.
- **Cells marked "Needs Confirmation"** reflect a genuine, flagged gap — not a guess. Do not treat silence as permission.

## Methodology & Limitations

- **Research method**: All 49 jurisdictions were researched via automated web search against primary government sources (state Departments of Revenue/Taxation, Comptrollers, ABC/tobacco-enforcement agencies), cross-checked against multiple independent queries per state. Direct page-fetching of `.gov` URLs was blocked by network policy in this research environment throughout — every finding, including this pass's corrections, relies on search-engine synthesis of primary-source pages (which does quote and cite the underlying `.gov`/statute text) rather than a raw fetch of the full page. **A compliance reviewer should still open each cited URL directly and confirm current text before relying on this document operationally** — this pass materially improved confidence on many rows, but did not add the ability to directly fetch pages.
- **Aggregator errors actively caught and corrected across both passes**: a claim that "Georgia bans internet tobacco sales" was traced to law governing the *country* of Georgia, not the U.S. state, and discarded; a Minnesota premium-cigar cap figure ($0.65) traced back to what appears to be an Ohio Department of Taxation source and remains unconfirmed after a second check; a North Carolina permit-fee claim ($400) could not be corroborated against the directly-quoted statutory fee ($25) and was discarded in favor of the statutory figure; a West Virginia "51%" and "7%" rate claim were both superseded by the confirmed current 12% statute text; a Maryland "70% flat rate" claim was corrected to a three-tier structure (30% OTP / 15% premium cigars / 70% other cigars) once the actual bulletin was reviewed. This is exactly the class of error this project was commissioned to avoid.
- **The $10-wholesale illustrative benchmark** is a modeling convenience for sorting only. Recompute actual tax owed against each SKU's real wholesale/invoice cost before pricing or remitting tax in any state.
- **Rates change frequently.** Confirmed changes found during this research window (2025–2026) include: Maine (43%→75%), Illinois (36%→45%, with a further change effective 2027), Ohio ($0.65→$0.67 cap), Kentucky (15%→6% premium cigar, signed law), Nevada (new remote-seller law), Nebraska (new remote-seller law, effective 2027), South Dakota (new second license, effective 2027), Colorado (rate increase effective 2027), Utah (DTC ban repeal, effective 2027), Louisiana (temporary premium-cigar cap through 2027), New Jersey (rate-hike bill confirmed dead), and pending/unresolved bills in Delaware, Connecticut, and Tennessee. **Re-pull this entire document shortly before any actual state-by-state launch — do not treat this as a static reference.**
- **Efficacy scores are directional judgment calls**, not a validated model. They do not incorporate actual sales data, shipping/logistics cost, competitor density, or state-specific marketing restrictions beyond what's noted above.
