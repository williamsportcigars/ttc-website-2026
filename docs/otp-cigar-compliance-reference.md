# OTP Cigar Compliance Reference — State-by-State (Lower 48 + DC)

**⚠️ COMPLIANCE RESEARCH DRAFT — NOT LEGAL ADVICE.**
This document is a research draft compiled for planning purposes only. It is **not** a legal opinion and must **not** be relied upon to file any state registration, tax account, or license application. Tobacco tax and delivery-sale law changes frequently, and a small number of items below remain marked **"Needs Confirmation"** because they could not be verified against a directly-fetched primary source (see *Methodology & Limitations* at the bottom). **Get sign-off from a tobacco-compliance attorney or compliance service before filing any state registration, tax account, or license application on the strength of this document.**

Prepared for: The Tobacco Center (Williamsport, PA) — planning for CONUS (lower 48 + DC) direct-to-consumer cigar shipping. PA is the home state and already holds a PA OTP license; PA is included below as a baseline reference, not as a target for "expansion."

**Revision note (2026‑09‑28, third pass):** two verification passes on 2026‑09‑28 worked through nearly every remaining "Needs Confirmation" flag by finding each state's actual delivery‑sale/mail‑order statute (the same pattern that already resolved Arizona, Oregon, Georgia, Indiana, Virginia, Wisconsin, Missouri, and North Carolina). Of the 20 states still flagged after the second pass, **17 are now resolved**: 15 flip to confirmed **Legal** (Florida, Alabama, Louisiana, Michigan, Minnesota, South Carolina, Kentucky, Tennessee, West Virginia, Mississippi, Wyoming, Delaware, New Jersey, Massachusetts, Maine), Illinois' *current* (pre‑2027) regime is confirmed permissive, and Connecticut's *current* law is confirmed to restrict only cigarettes (its cigar risk is entirely in a still‑pending bill). **Iowa flips the other way — confirmed banned.** The standout finding: **Massachusetts requires no state license at all for a no‑premises internet cigar retailer shipping to individual MA residents** — Mass DOR's own guidance says the excise liability shifts to the consumer in that scenario. Only **New Hampshire** and **Oklahoma** remain genuinely unresolved.

---

## How to read this table

- **Sort order**: ascending by **illustrative effective cigar tax**, calculated on a **hypothetical $10.00 wholesale-price premium cigar** so that percentage-of-wholesale, percentage-with-cap, and flat-per-cigar tax structures can be compared on one axis. This is a modeling simplification — always recompute against actual invoice cost.
- **Efficacy score (1–9)**: my own judgment call, not a formula output. It weighs confirmed DTC legality, licensing cost/complexity, the tax burden, and rough market-size potential for slow, zero-paid-advertising sales.
- **Cells marked "Needs Confirmation"** reflect a genuine, flagged gap — not a guess.

## Methodology & Limitations

- **Research method**: All 49 jurisdictions were researched via automated web search against primary government sources, cross-checked across multiple independent queries per state. Direct page-fetching of `.gov` URLs (and, on request, DuckDuckGo's own search results page) was tested and confirmed blocked by network policy throughout this research environment — every finding relies on search-engine synthesis of primary-source pages rather than a raw fetch of the full page. **A compliance reviewer should still open each cited URL directly and confirm current text before relying on this document operationally.**
- **The research pattern that resolved most of the remaining gaps**: rather than searching generically for "is DTC legal in [state]," searching for each state's specific *delivery-sale* or *mail-order tobacco* statute (the dedicated legal category nearly every state has, separate from its general retail-licensing law) reliably surfaced either an explicit cigar carve-out, an explicit cigar inclusion, or — in Iowa's case — an explicit ban. This is the same method that resolved Arizona, Oregon, Georgia, Indiana, Virginia, Wisconsin, Missouri, and North Carolina in earlier passes.
- **Aggregator errors actively caught and corrected across all passes**: "Georgia bans internet tobacco sales" traced to law governing the *country* of Georgia, not the U.S. state; a Minnesota $0.65 cap figure traced to an apparent Ohio source and remains unconfirmed; a North Carolina $400 permit-fee claim was discarded in favor of the statutory $25 figure; West Virginia's "51%"/"7%" claims were superseded by the confirmed current 12% statute text; Maryland's "70% flat rate" was corrected to a three-tier structure once the actual bulletin was reviewed.
- **The $10-wholesale illustrative benchmark** is a modeling convenience for sorting only. Recompute actual tax owed against each SKU's real wholesale/invoice cost before pricing or remitting tax in any state.
- **Rates and rules change frequently.** Re-pull this entire document shortly before any actual state-by-state launch — do not treat this as a static reference.
- **Efficacy scores are directional judgment calls**, not a validated model.
