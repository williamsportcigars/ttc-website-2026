# OTP Cigar Compliance Reference — State-by-State (Lower 48 + DC)

**⚠️ COMPLIANCE RESEARCH DRAFT — NOT LEGAL ADVICE.**
This document is a research draft compiled for planning purposes only. It is **not** a legal opinion and must **not** be relied upon to file any state registration, tax account, or license application. Tobacco tax and delivery-sale law changes frequently (several states changed rates or rules in 2025–2026 alone, and more changes are already scheduled for 2027), and a meaningful share of the findings below are marked **"Needs Confirmation"** because they could not be verified against a directly-fetched primary source in this research pass (see *Methodology & Limitations* at the bottom). **Get sign-off from a tobacco-compliance attorney or compliance service before filing any state registration, tax account, or license application on the strength of this document.**

Prepared for: The Tobacco Center (Williamsport, PA) — planning for CONUS (lower 48 + DC) direct-to-consumer cigar shipping. PA is the home state and already holds a PA OTP license; PA is included below as a baseline reference, not as a target for "expansion."

---

## How to read this table

- **Sort order**: ascending by **illustrative effective cigar tax**, calculated on a **hypothetical $10.00 wholesale-price premium cigar** (a mid-to-upper-range premium cigar) so that percentage-of-wholesale, percentage-with-cap, and flat-per-cigar tax structures can be compared on one axis. This is a modeling simplification — real tax owed depends on the actual wholesale price of each SKU, and a handful of states use a different basis (e.g., landed/invoice cost, "actual cost," or a retail-price threshold) that is only approximately equivalent to "wholesale price" for this purpose. Always recompute against actual invoice cost.
- **Efficacy score (1–9)**: my own judgment call, not a formula output. It weighs: (a) whether DTC shipping is confirmed legal, banned, or ambiguous; (b) licensing cost and complexity (fee, bond size, number of agencies, physical-presence requirements); (c) the tax burden from the column to its left; and (d) rough market-size potential for **slow, organic, zero-paid-advertising sales** (bigger population ≈ more plausible organic/word-of-mouth volume even with no marketing spend). It does **not** account for actual demand data, competitor density, or shipping/logistics cost — treat it as a prioritization starting point, not a verdict.
- **Cells marked "Needs Confirmation"** reflect a genuine, flagged gap in this research pass — not a guess. Do not treat silence as permission.

## Methodology & Limitations

- **Research method**: All 49 jurisdictions were researched via automated web search against primary government sources (state Departments of Revenue/Taxation, Comptrollers, ABC/tobacco-enforcement agencies), cross-checked against multiple independent queries per state. In several research sessions, direct page-fetching of the underlying `.gov` URLs was blocked by network policy in the research environment; in those cases, findings rely on search-engine synthesis of primary-source pages rather than a human or model directly reading the raw statute/bulletin text end-to-end. Every row's Source column cites the specific `.gov` page(s) identified as authoritative — **a compliance reviewer should still open each cited URL directly and confirm current text before relying on this document operationally.**
- **Aggregator errors actively caught and corrected during this research**: a claim that "Georgia bans internet tobacco sales" was traced to law governing the *country* of Georgia, not the U.S. state, and discarded; a Minnesota premium-cigar cap figure ($0.65) traced back to what appears to be an Ohio Department of Taxation source and is flagged as unconfirmed rather than used; a North Carolina permit-fee claim ($400) could not be corroborated against the directly-quoted statutory fee ($25) and was discarded in favor of the statutory figure. This is exactly the class of error this project was commissioned to avoid — treat any figure in this document that still carries a "needs confirmation" flag with real skepticism.
- **The $10-wholesale illustrative benchmark** is a modeling convenience for sorting only. Recompute actual tax owed against each SKU's real wholesale/invoice cost before pricing or remitting tax in any state.
- **Rates change frequently.** Confirmed changes found during this research window (2025–2026) include: Maine (43%→75%), Illinois (36%→45%, with a further change effective 2027), Ohio ($0.65→$0.67 cap), Nevada (new remote-seller law), Nebraska (new remote-seller law, effective 2027), South Dakota (new second license, effective 2027), Colorado (rate increase effective 2027), Utah (DTC ban repeal, effective 2027), Louisiana (temporary premium-cigar cap through 2027), and pending/unresolved bills in Delaware, New Jersey, Connecticut, Tennessee, New York, and West Virginia. **Re-pull this entire document shortly before any actual state-by-state launch — do not treat this as a static reference.**
- **Efficacy scores are directional judgment calls**, not a validated model. They do not incorporate actual sales data, shipping/logistics cost, competitor density, or state-specific marketing restrictions beyond what's noted above.

**Dates pulled**: 2026‑09‑19 (initial 44 jurisdictions) and 2026‑09‑27 (District of Columbia; North Carolina, North Dakota, Ohio, Oklahoma, Oregon — researched in a follow-up pass after the first pass exhausted its search budget before reaching them).
